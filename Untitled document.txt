On the first of September 1939, German troops invaded Poland. Just two days later, Britain and France declared war. But what caused this devastating conflict? Was it resentment from World War I, diplomatic failure, or Hitler's deliberate ambitions? Although the Treaty of Versailles, economic instability and appeasement contributed to rising tensions, Nazi Germany was responsible to a very great extent for causing World War II, particularly in Europe. Other factors created opportunities for conflict, but Hitler's expansionist ideology and calculated aggression transformed these tensions into war.






To begin with, the Treaty of Versailles and the Great Depression enabled Hitler's rise, but their responsibility for war was indirect. Article 231 of the 1919 Treaty of Versailles assigned Germany and its allies responsibility for wartime losses and damage. As an official peace settlement, the Treaty provides reliable evidence of the conditions imposed on Germany. However, it cannot reveal how Germans responded or prove that another war was inevitable. Hitler exploited resentment towards the Treaty, while the Great Depression weakened confidence in democracy and increased support for extremism. However, these circumstances explain why Hitler gained support, not why he chose conquest. The Treaty did not require Germany to invade neighbouring countries; Hitler deliberately used national grievances to justify expansion. Therefore, Versailles and economic instability contributed to the conditions for conflict, but Germany bore greater responsibility for the aggressive decisions that followed.






More significantly, Hitler's expansionist ideology and deliberate territorial aggression demonstrate Germany's greater responsibility for the outbreak of war in Europe. A photograph from the 15th of March 1939, preserved by the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, shows German tanks entering Czechoslovakia. As contemporary visual evidence, it reliably documents military occupation, although it cannot independently reveal Hitler's motives. Hitler claimed that his expansion was about uniting German-speaking populations. However, Hitler's occupation of predominantly Czech territory exposed his true intentions: territorial expansion rather than simply national reunification. If Hitler merely wanted to reverse Versailles, why occupy territory where most people were not even German? Considered alongside Nazi expansionist ideology, the photograph demonstrates deliberate territorial aggression rather than a defensive response to international pressures. Thus, although Versailles created resentment, Hitler's willingness to violate agreements and threaten neighbouring states made Germany substantially more responsible for escalating tensions towards war.




Nevertheless, although appeasement and Soviet cooperation facilitated German aggression, Germany remained responsible for the decisions that ultimately triggered war in Europe. A photograph from the 30th of September 1938, held by the Imperial War Museums, shows British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain addressing crowds after returning from Munich. It reflects Britain's optimism that conceding the Sudetenland would prevent war. However, while valuable for understanding the public presentation of appeasement, it cannot establish Hitler's intentions. Appeasement failed to restrain aggression; Germany deliberately committed it. The Secret Additional Protocol to the Nazi–Soviet Pact, signed on 23 August 1939, provides direct evidence of Germany's territorial planning. It established German and Soviet spheres of influence in Poland and questioned whether an independent Polish state should continue to exist. As a confidential agreement, it reveals territorial intentions concealed from the public. Soviet cooperation also reduced Germany's immediate risk of conflict with the USSR over Poland, making invasion more feasible. Nevertheless, Germany independently chose to launch the attack. Germany's subsequent invasion of Poland on 1 September directly triggered Britain's and France's declarations of war. In his radio address on the 3rd of September, Chamberlain argued that peace remained possible, but “Hitler would not have it. He had evidently made up his mind to attack Poland”. Although Chamberlain had political reasons to justify Britain's declaration of war, Germany's invasion and the secret protocol independently support his accusation that Hitler deliberately pursued aggression. Therefore, while appeasement and Soviet cooperation contributed to the crisis, Germany's calculated invasion was the decisive cause of war in Europe.


So, to what extent was Germany responsible for causing World War II? To a very great extent, particularly in Europe.   Japan's invasion of China in 1937 demonstrates that major conflict had already begun in Asia independently of Germany, limiting the extent to which Hitler alone can be blamed for a global war. However, these wider conflicts and international tensions did not make a European war inevitable. Hitler's expansionist ambitions, violation of the Munich settlement and deliberate invasion of Poland demonstrate that Germany made the decisive choices that brought Europe into war. Other factors created the conditions for conflict, but Germany made the decisions that turned those conditions into war.
